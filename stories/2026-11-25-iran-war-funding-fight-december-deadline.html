Democrats Threaten to Defund Troops Mid-War to Score Political Points

Senate Democrats are playing dangerous political games with American troops in harm's way, threatening to block critical war funding in a transparent attempt to embarrass President Trump and hand Iran a propaganda victory. With U.S. forces engaged in active operations against Iranian military targets, progressive senators are openly discussing using the December 11 government funding deadline as leverage to cut off resources for the mission—a move that would embolden Tehran and put servicemembers at risk.

The issue, Republicans argue, is fundamentally about supporting our troops and investing in our military, as Sen. Jim Banks told Fox News Digital. The Pentagon has requested $200 billion in supplemental funding to replenish depleted munitions stockpiles and maintain operational readiness—investments that will create American manufacturing jobs and reduce dependence on foreign suppliers.

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth defended the funding request with characteristic bluntness during a Pentagon press briefing, telling reporters that it takes money to kill bad guys. The straightforward assessment resonated with conservatives who see the conflict as a necessary confrontation with a regime that has spent decades sponsoring terrorism, developing nuclear weapons, and threatening American allies.

Even some Republicans, however, have grown skeptical of the mounting costs and mission creep. Rep. Rich McCormick of Georgia, when asked by the Washington Examiner about spending offsets for the supplemental request, offered a terse response: It's war. The comment reflected a growing unease among fiscal conservatives about open-ended commitments, even as most Republicans remain united in opposing Democratic efforts to defund operations.

The internal tensions on the right became impossible to ignore when Fox & Friends co-hosts clashed on-air over the war's impact on the Republican agenda. Rachel Campos-Duffy argued that Trump's decision to launch the conflict had cost us the Trump agenda, sadly, pointing to the administration's stalled domestic priorities and the GOP's disastrous midterm results.

Tucker Carlson went even further, declaring in a blistering attack that the main lobby for this war was Fox News. The former primetime host accused his old network's biggest stars of having personally persuaded Trump to launch the strikes, a charge that exposed deep fissures within the conservative movement over foreign intervention.

Still, most Republicans maintain that regardless of how the conflict began, pulling funding now would be catastrophic. Senate Appropriations Chair Susan Collins is reportedly negotiating a compromise that would fund munitions replenishment while blocking offensive operations unless Trump secures formal congressional authorization—a middle ground that acknowledges constitutional concerns without abandoning deployed forces.

The fundamental question for Republicans is whether backing away from the Iran mission represents prudent restraint or dangerous weakness. Democrats may frame their defunding efforts as constitutional principle, but conservatives see political opportunism that emboldens America's enemies and betrays the men and women in uniform who answered the call to serve.

An Unauthorized $48 Billion War Americans Never Voted For — And Congress Can't Stop It

Senate Democrats are preparing a historic confrontation with President Trump over his illegal and catastrophically expensive war with Iran, threatening to withhold funding for offensive operations in the December 11 spending bill unless the administration obtains proper congressional authorization. The conflict has already cost American taxpayers nearly $50 billion, killed more than 20 U.S. service members, driven up gas and grocery prices, and achieved none of its stated objectives—yet Trump demands another $200 billion to continue the quagmire.

Sen. Tim Kaine, who is leading the Democratic effort to attach war-ending language to the continuing resolution, didn't mince words about the conflict's strategic bankruptcy. This is a dumb war, Kaine told CNN, adding with exasperation: "I mean, have we really learned nothing?" The Virginia senator emphasized that Democrats are prepared to fund defense robustly but will not bankroll a war that is both illegal and despised by the public.

The emerging Democratic strategy would trade supplemental funding for certain defense priorities in exchange for a prohibition on further Iran war expenditures. As Kaine explained to CNN, Democrats are willing to continue a war that's illegal, and the American public hates—and if Republicans force a shutdown over the issue, that decision will be entirely on them.

Sen. Bernie Sanders has been equally forceful in condemning the conflict, calling it an illegal, unconstitutional and horrific war in an interview with Fox News Digital. Sanders made clear that the American people want to see it ended immediately—a sentiment backed by polling showing overwhelming public opposition to the conflict and widespread anger over its economic consequences.

Maryland Sen. Chris Van Hollen, who is challenging Chuck Schumer for Democratic leadership and spearheading the defunding push alongside Sanders, Tammy Duckworth, and Michael Bennet, framed the December 11 deadline as a moment of accountability. Van Hollen warned that if Trump wants to shut down the government to continue conducting an illegal and senseless war in Iran, that choice will be his alone.

Sen. Kirsten Gillibrand announced legislation with Tammy Duckworth and Elizabeth Warren that would prohibit funding for offensive operations while allowing resources for force protection and defensive measures. Gillibrand emphasized that Democrats intend to use the power of the purse to end what she called an illegal, unauthorized, immoral war, citing the midterm wave election as a clear mandate from voters.

In the House, progressives are equally determined to block further war funding. Rep. Ro Khanna of California told HuffPost that Democrats are going to fight to get that Iran funding removed for offensive military capability. Rep.-elect Richard Ojeda of North Carolina went further, arguing that in this conflict, we're not the good guys here—a stark moral indictment of the entire enterprise.

The Democratic unity on this issue stands in remarkable contrast to the fractures visible on the Republican side, where even Fox News hosts are now openly clashing over whether the war was a catastrophic mistake that destroyed Trump's domestic agenda and cost the GOP its congressional majority.

For Democrats, the December showdown represents more than a funding fight—it's a constitutional moment that will determine whether Congress can still check an imperial presidency, or whether American wars will continue to be launched, escalated, and prolonged without meaningful legislative consent or accountability to voters.

Fact Check

Separating Fact from Spin

Both sides are presenting selective versions of a genuine constitutional and fiscal crisis. Here is what we actually know about the Iran war funding battle and what each side is leaving out.

THE UNDISPUTED FACTS:

✓ The $48.3 billion price tag is real: Pentagon budget official Arthur Miller testified to the Senate Armed Services Committee that the Iran conflict had cost an estimated $48.3 billion through November 13, with the administration requesting an additional $200 billion in supplemental funding.

✓ A bipartisan deal is emerging: Senate Appropriations Chair Susan Collins, a Republican, told reporters she is eyeing a potential deal that would provide funds to restock depleted munitions but block further funding for offensive operations unless President Trump obtains formal congressional authorization—suggesting this is not a purely partisan issue.

✓ The December 11 deadline is real: Congress must pass a spending bill by that date to avoid a government shutdown, and both parties acknowledge the Iran war funding question will be central to those negotiations.

✓ No congressional authorization exists: President Trump launched the Iran strikes without seeking an Authorization for Use of Military Force from Congress, relying instead on executive authority—a decision that legal scholars across the spectrum have questioned given the scale and duration of the conflict.

CONSERVATIVE SPIN VS. REALITY:

⚠️ Claim: Democrats are defunding troops in the middle of combat. Reality: The Democratic proposals would continue funding for force protection and defensive operations while blocking new offensive strikes. This is functionally identical to what Republican Sen. Susan Collins is proposing in her bipartisan compromise. Framing it as abandoning troops in combat is rhetoric, not substance.

⚠️ Claim: The funding is about supporting our military. Reality: Much of the requested $200 billion would go to munitions manufacturers and defense contractors, not directly to service member pay or benefits. The "supporting our troops" frame obscures the larger question of whether the war itself serves American interests.

✓ Fair Point: Pulling funding during active operations does create operational challenges. Military commanders have legitimate concerns about planning and execution when funding becomes uncertain. The conservative argument that Congress should have debated authorization before operations began, rather than threatening to defund mid-conflict, has constitutional merit.

LIBERAL SPIN VS. REALITY:

⚠️ Claim: This is an entirely illegal war. Reality: While the lack of congressional authorization is constitutionally problematic, presidents of both parties have conducted military operations without explicit AUMFs for decades, relying on Article II commander-in-chief powers and older authorizations. The Iran conflict raises serious legal questions, but calling it unambiguously illegal oversimplifies complex War Powers Act jurisprudence.

⚠️ Claim: The war has achieved none of its objectives. Reality: This depends entirely on what the objectives were. If the goal was regime change, that clearly hasn't happened. If the goal was degrading Iranian military capabilities and deterring attacks on U.S. allies, the assessment is more complicated. The liberal framing assumes total failure without acknowledging any operational successes.

✓ Fair Point: The constitutional process has been badly undermined. Regardless of one's view on the war's merits, the framers gave Congress the power to declare war for a reason. A conflict of this scale, cost, and duration without formal legislative authorization represents a serious erosion of constitutional checks and balances. The liberal argument that Congress must reassert its authority has significant constitutional foundation.

THE BIGGER PICTURE:

The Iran funding battle exposes a fundamental tension in American governance that neither party wants to fully address: the presidency has accumulated vast unilateral war-making powers that the Constitution never intended, and Congress has been complicit in surrendering those powers for decades because taking responsibility for war and peace decisions is politically risky.

Both parties selectively invoke constitutional principles when it suits them. Republicans who now defend executive war powers supported legislative restrictions during the Obama administration. Democrats who demand congressional authorization now were often silent when previous Democratic presidents conducted military operations without explicit legislative approval.

The debate over whether to fund this specific conflict has become a proxy war for larger unresolved questions: What level of threat justifies military action? What constitutes meaningful congressional authorization? How much deference do courts owe the political branches on war powers questions? These are questions the political system has avoided answering clearly for 75 years.

The Bottom Line: This is a genuine constitutional crisis wrapped in partisan theater. The war's $48 billion price tag is real, the lack of congressional authorization is constitutionally problematic, and the December 11 deadline will force both parties to decide whether they're willing to trigger a government shutdown over the issue. What's missing from both narratives is an honest reckoning with how American democracy arrived at a place where wars can be launched, escalated, and sustained for months without meaningful legislative debate—and whether either party is actually committed to changing that system.
